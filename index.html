PBS Wisconsin AI Support Assistant

A customer support tool we built with PBS Wisconsin's Audience Services team, to help staff answer viewer questions quickly and point to where each answer came from.

I was the team's Scrum Master and also wrote code for the app. I ran most of our meetings with our partners at PBS Wisconsin, which meant listening carefully to what they inteded for the App and communicating clearly the successes, challenges, and progress we faced while building the App over the semester.

The problem

The Audience Services staff field a lot of calls and emails about PBS Passport, streaming devices, live TV and member accounts. The answers exist, but they're spread across hundreds of help articles on three different sites. What they wanted was a faster way to find the right one. What they really didn't want was an AI that sounds confident about a policy it made up, so that concern shaped most of our design decisions.

What it does

A staff member types in the viewer's question and gets a short summary with steps, where every claim links back to the help article it came from. Clicking a citation shows the exact passage, who owns the page and when it was last updated, so staff can check it themselves.

The part I'm happiest with is how it behaves when it isn't sure. If the search can't back up an answer, it says "No verified answer" and shows the closest pages instead of guessing. Questions about billing, eligibility or availability always need a source. Staff can also log those gaps so the help content can improve over time.

A few smaller things came straight out of conversations with the team at PBS: a "phone script" button that rewrites an answer into something you can read aloud on a call (keeping the same sources), a one-click email reply, and follow-up questions that remember context, so "what about Roku?" still means Passport on Roku.

How it works

  1. A crawler saves the public help pages and, on later runs, only fetches what changed. A typical refresh takes about 17 requests.
  2. Pages are split into roughly 200-word passages and embedded with a small model that runs locally on the CPU, so this part costs nothing to run.
  3. Each question is searched two ways, by meaning and by exact keywords (which helps with error messages and device names), and the two rankings are merged.
  4. Claude writes the answer in a fixed structure that lists which passages it used.
  5. Before anything reaches the screen, the backend checks those citations against what Claude was actually given and fills in the real text and links itself. If nothing checks out, the answer becomes "no answer." This turned out to be the simplest way to make sure the model can't cite a source that doesn't exist.

How we worked

We wrote a short spec for each feature and reviewed it together before anyone started building, which saved us from a lot of rework. Work was planned in sprints, and every merge request ran the tests and a production build automatically. The login screen is only a design for now; connecting it to PBS Wisconsin's single sign-on would be the next step, along with a few other things we ran out of time for.

Fantasy Trail Stitcher

Walk a legendary journey at its real length, a few kilometers at a time near home. Each walk is a real route that matches the distance you pick, sticking to trails where it can.

Imagine going on the same journey as Frodo. From the Shire to Moria, from Moria to Mount Doom. This would be awesome! While you can't walk the exact route, how about walking the distance little by little every day in your own city!

When a walk reaches a stop on the journey, the route bends through a real place that fits it. From the Arboretum, the Dead Marshes land in Gardner Marsh, and from campus, Lothlórien lands in Muir Woods. It works in any US city, too: the app downloads that city's map the first time someone walks there and saves it for next time.

Underneath all of that is a routing problem that turned out to be more interesting than I expected. Finding the shortest path between two points is a solved problem. Finding a path of a specific length is a lot harder, and most of this project is me working out why.

0 → 90%+loop success from an urban start after the bridge fix
49 / 50loops of 3–20 km within tolerance
< 0.7 smedian time to plan a 20 km loop

What I tried, in order

  1. First tryI started by listing the shortest paths in order (Yen's algorithm) and picking the first one long enough. It stalled quickly, since the first 50 paths were all barely longer than the shortest one.
  2. BetterNext I built the path step by step with a randomized search that backs up at dead ends, skipping any turn that couldn't reach the destination in the distance left. That got much further.
  3. What stuckVery long routes still failed sometimes, and pulling in more map data didn't help. That's because finding the longest route between two points is a famously hard (NP-hard) problem. Lots of short attempts ended up working better than one long one.

Trails, roads and loops

One surprise was how little of the map is actually trail: around 83% road and under 5% trail, in thousands of disconnected pieces. So instead of banning roads, the search just prefers trails when it has a choice. Loops brought their own puzzle. A quarter of all segments are dead ends like cul-de-sacs and driveways, which a loop can never use without backtracking. Filtering those out up front took the success rate from an urban start from zero to over 90%.

Stops on real places

Each stop on a journey has a kind of terrain, like forest, water, marsh, hill or bridge, with fallbacks when there's nothing close (a hill falls back to a tower, then to woods). A walk that reaches a stop is planned in legs: out to a matching place at about the right distance, then on to the finish, without reusing any stretch from an earlier leg. If nothing fits, the stop goes at its proportional point on a normal route instead. I decided early that the walk's length should always come before the scenery, since that's the part people are actually counting on.

Other cities

Pulling a new city's map takes a few minutes, and almost all of that is waiting on the free public OpenStreetMap service. Decorah, Iowa took about 4 minutes to download and under 2 seconds to build. The page shows a "preparing" state while it waits, and each city only has to be downloaded once.

There's still plenty I'd like to improve. Progress is saved in the browser, so it doesn't follow you to another device, and stops can only land on places OpenStreetMap has mapped.

Send It

A simple tracker for rock climbers to keep a log of the routes they've attempted, are projecting, or have sent.

Most climbers I know keep this list in their heads, or in a notes app that gets messy fast. Send It lets you log a route with its grade, style and notes, mark it as unclimbed, a project or sent, and filter by where you're at. A few stats at the top update as you go.

I built it for a user interface course, so I spent most of my time on the small details: keeping components reusable, making the layout calm and readable, and making sure it worked well on a phone at the crag.

Machine learning · 2025

Video Game Poster Genre Matcher

A group project asking a simple question: how much can a model tell about a game's genre just from its cover art?

Stack
TensorFlowKerasscikit-learnJupyter
Team
Group project
Links
You can view the Notebooks on GitHub to see the algorithms in action

Cover art is designed to tell you what kind of game you're looking at, so we wondered how much of that a model could pick up across ten genres, from Action and Role-Playing to Puzzle and Simulation.

We started with a simple logistic regression as a baseline, then trained a small convolutional network from scratch, and finally tried transfer learning with pretrained models (MobileNetV2, EfficientNetB0 and VGG). Looking at top-3 accuracy and the confusion matrices was the most interesting part, since the mistakes were often understandable ones between genres whose covers really do look alike.
